SOTA视频模型的物理表现还有提升空间

如果视频生成模型要作为 world model 用于预测、规划或具身交互，不能只看视觉保真度和时序连续性，还要看物理能力。

Einsia AI发布了 World Models' Last Exam in Physics——视频模型生成的画面好看，物理上如何？

40 个受控任务，作者用八个视频模型一共生成了 1,280 段视频。先看物体和事件在视频里是否始终可观测；再从视频中提取运动轨迹、角度、周期、液面高度和预先设定做对照。

综合得分最高的 Seedance 2.5 只有 57.76/100。“含石头的冰块融化”任务平均 3.45 分。光反射和带电小球平衡两个任务两极分化：有的模型接近满分，有的连 15 分都没。

论文的综合分数把一致性和物理测量分开。设 C(V) 为时序一致性与可观测性分数，P(V) 为物理测量分数：

\[S(V)=0.15C(V)+0.85\widetilde P(V),\qquad \widetilde P(V)=\begin{cases}P(V),&C(V)\geq80,\\0,&C(V)<80.\end{cases}\]

只有通过一致性门控，物理分数才会计入综合分数。这说明时序检查解决的是“能不能测”，不直接说明“物理上对不对”。

自由落体中，所有视频都通过了时序一致性检查，画面连贯，物体也没有凭空消失，但跨模型平均分只有 26.61。看起来连贯和符合重力完全是两回事。

对于其中的抛体运动，论文会比较高度 H 与水平射程 R 的关系：

\[r_{\mathrm{spatial}}=\left|\frac{H}{R}-\frac14\right|.\]

现有的许多实验结论已经说明画面变好不会自动带来物理能力。原因也许是数据和训练目标：视频数量庞大但很多缺少运动和系统性的干预数据，比如同一个物体在不同质量、速度、受力和环境下分别会怎样。现在很多方法主要围绕时序连续、画面保持度，对物理守恒关系和长期因果的约束还很弱。

在业界，模型+数据变大后，物理得分确实有稳定上升。但保持其他不变，完全靠堆参数和数据真的是最好的解决办法吗？

对学界大部分团队来说，现实困境是在大模型上验证方法等所需的算力几乎不可得，能做的也许只能是一个新 benchmark 或者一小组高质量的反事实数据。

不知学界论文爆炸的当下，真正能被 scale 的新方法在哪里？

SOTA Video Models Still Have Room to Improve in Physical Consistency

If video generation models are to serve as world models for prediction, planning, or embodied interaction, evaluation must go beyond visual fidelity and temporal continuity. The generated sequence must also reproduce measurable physical relationships under controlled conditions.

The Einsia AI team recently released the preprint World Models' Last Exam in Physics. Instead of asking a vision-language model to directly judge whether a video “looks physical,” the benchmark defines 40 controlled tasks across nine physical families, including mechanics, optics, fluids, phase transitions, electromagnetism, and surface tension. Eight video models generate four samples per task, producing 1,280 videos in total.

Each task specifies an initial image, a video prompt, experimental assumptions, and measurable physical criteria. The evaluator first checks whether objects remain identifiable, required events occur, and trajectories can be recovered. It then uses tracking, segmentation, geometric fitting, region-of-interest photometry, and temporal analysis to extract positions, angles, periods, trajectories, and liquid levels. The physical test does not require a reference video; it checks whether observables recovered from the generated sequence satisfy the predefined relationship.

The paper separates evidence availability from physical correctness. Let (C(V)) denote temporal consistency and task observability, and (P(V)) the physical measurement score:

\[S(V)=0.15C(V)+0.85\widetilde P(V),\qquad \widetilde P(V)=\begin{cases}P(V),&C(V)\geq80,\\0,&C(V)<80.\end{cases}\]

Passing (C(V)\geq80) therefore means that the video has enough evidence to enter the physical evaluation. It does not establish that the physical relationship is correct.

The results are weak. The best overall score is only 57.76/100. The task “melting ice containing a stone” has a model-averaged score of 3.45, and all eight models receive zero physical scores there; the nonzero composite values mainly come from the consistency term. Light reflection and charged-sphere equilibrium show strong model-to-model separation: the best results are close to 96 and 98, while the other models reach no more than about 50 and 15.

Free fall makes the gap especially clear. Every available video passes the automatic consistency gate, yet the cross-model average score is only 26.61. The sequence can be continuous and the object can remain visible while the trajectory still violates the quantitative relation implied by gravity. For a 45-degree projectile returning to its launch height, the benchmark evaluates the residual

\[r_{\mathrm{spatial}}=\left|\frac{H}{R}-\frac14\right|.\]

The ratio cancels the initial speed and gravitational acceleration and avoids absolute spatial calibration, but it still depends on an appropriate side-view geometry and does not remove perspective distortion.

The benchmark exposes several distinct failure modes: the required event does not occur, the target object cannot be observed reliably, the event occurs but its geometric or dynamical relation is wrong, or constraints drift during a multi-step process. The benchmark measures observable physical consistency under stated assumptions; it does not certify complete physical understanding or unmeasured properties.

Data and objectives are plausible explanations. Internet videos are mostly passive observations. They rarely vary mass, initial velocity, applied force, boundary conditions, or material properties in a controlled way, and they seldom provide paired counterfactual trajectories. More useful training data for physical world models may be intervention-based spatiotemporal sequences with controlled variables, state trajectories, event labels, and failure evidence. Objectives may also need to include trajectory constraints, event ordering, conservation relations, and long-horizon rollout stability in addition to pixel or feature similarity.

A single cross-sectional benchmark is not enough to establish a stable physical scaling law. To test whether increasing model, data, and compute scale is the best path, researchers would need controlled comparisons of model size, training tokens, compute, and physical scores, together with category-level gains. Otherwise, an increase in score can still reflect changes in architecture, data mixture, or inference settings.

For resource-constrained teams, the entry point need not be a larger base model. Measurement-driven rewards, intervention and counterfactual data, physics-constrained post-training, and representations that transfer across tasks may all be scalable directions. “Scalable” should mean that a method improves with data or compute and remains effective across models, tasks, and longer closed-loop rollouts. The important question after the rapid growth of papers is which methods can transfer gains from one benchmark to broader physical world modeling.
